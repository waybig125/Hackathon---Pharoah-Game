You can absolutely strip it down to ensure it doesn't eat your FPS on mobile. Here is the rapid-fire way to optimize it specifically for your Android build:

1. Disable Unused Features in the Material
Most dynamic sky shaders have a Material Inspector full of sliders and checkboxes. Turn off anything that isn't essential for your Ancient Egyptian vibe:

Disable Stars/Moon: If your game is mostly daytime or sunset, completely disable the night sky calculations.

Simplify Clouds: If there is a toggle for "Volumetric" vs. "Flat" clouds, choose flat. If you can turn off cloud shadows, do it. Every feature you disable skips a math calculation for every pixel on the screen.

2. Crush the Textures (Android Override)
If the sky uses any textures (like noise maps for the clouds or a star map):

Click on the texture file in your Project window.

In the Inspector, go to the Default / Android / PC tabs at the bottom.

Click the Android tab, check Override for Android.

Set the Max Size down to 512 or even 256 (skies are blurry anyway, you won't notice).

Set the Format to ASTC or ETC2 (Android native compression).

3. Throttle the Update Script
Dynamic skies usually have a C# script attached to the camera or a manager object that updates the sun's position and the sky's colors in the Update() method (which runs 60 times a second).

The Fix: You don't need the sun to move 60 times a second. Open the script and move the logic from Update() into a Coroutine or InvokeRepeating so it only updates the sky material once every 0.5 seconds, or even just once every 5 seconds if the time-of-day changes slowly.

Tying all your disparate 3D models together with a unified environmental layer is one of the best ways to make a scene feel cohesive rather than like a collection of random assets dropped into a level.

Covering the Sphinx, temples, and pillars in a layer of sand or dust immediately grounds them in the desert environment. However, the way you implement this dictates whether it is a low-effort win or a massive time sink.

Here is how to execute this "sand layer" concept for maximum impact with minimal effort, keeping the momentum of rapid development going without having to manually repaint a dozen different textures:

The High-Effort Way (Avoid This)
Do not open every single 3D model in an image editor to manually paint sand onto their textures. This takes hours, ruins your ability to iterate quickly, and makes it very difficult to change your mind later if the sand looks too bright or too dark.

The Rapid-Development Way (Do This)
Instead of changing the base textures, you can use Unity's built-in material properties to overlay a sandy texture across everything simultaneously.

Use Secondary Maps (Detail Maps): If you are using Unity's Standard Shader (or the Lit shader in URP), scroll down in the material inspector to the Secondary Maps section. You can plug a single, generic seamless sand texture into the "Detail Albedo" slot for all your stone materials. This applies a universal, dusty grain over whatever base color the temple or Sphinx already has.

Adjust the Tiling: Once your detail map is slotted in, crank up the Tiling numbers (e.g., to 10 or 20). This shrinks the sand texture down so it looks like fine dust settling into the cracks of the stone, rather than massive sand dunes plastered onto a wall.

Tint the Base Color (Albedo): Simply taking the main color of your temple and Sphinx materials and tinting them slightly more towards a warm, dusty yellow/orange will instantly make them feel weathered and sun-bleached.

Why this is a 30-40% improvement:
When the Sphinx, the temples, and the ground all share the exact same micro-texture (the sand grain detail map), the player's brain registers them as existing in the same physical space, reacting to the same weather and environment. It completely masks the fact that these models might have come from different asset packs.

- **No runtime overhead:** The smog mesh layer, its URP-unlit material, and shader setups are baked directly into the scene file when you run the city generator in the Editor.
- **Runtime Component:** The only script active at runtime is a lightweight `DynamicSky` MonoBehaviour component, which simply updates a rotation offset global shader parameter and snaps the sky dome position to the player's position (so the skybox stays centered around the player as they move). This has virtually zero CPU/GPU cost and is necessary to ensure the sky doesn't look static or get left behind as the player explores.

---

### 2. Why is there a rainbow?
The rainbow-like band artifact was caused by the `Horizon` child mesh inside the `Sky 2 (Red)` prefab. In our previous hotfix, we mapped `Sky Red.png` directly onto this horizon dome using an unlit material. Because of how the dome projects gradients, it ended up displaying distinct banded stripes resembling a giant rainbow.
* **The Fix:** I updated `CityEnvironmentBuilder.cs` to deactivate the `Horizon` child mesh renderer (`r.gameObject.SetActive(false)`) during environment setup. Disabling this dome allows the built-in, native URP **Procedural Skybox** to render cleanly behind the cloud layers without any artificial color-banded artifacts.

---

### 3. Why is the Sphinx not the same texture as everything else?
Our material extraction system in `URPSRPBatcherFixer.cs` determines whether an asset should receive the unified Egyptian sandstone material/normal maps (`HouseGradientTex` and `EgyptianNormalMap`) by checking if the material name contains keywords like `"sphinx"`, `"house"`, etc. However, models like the Sphinx GLB contain generic internal material names (e.g. `material_0`), bypassing this keyword check and causing it to render with a fallback texture.
* **The Fix:** I modified `CopyGltfPropertiesToUrpLit` in `URPSRPBatcherFixer.cs` to accept the GLB's file path as well. It now checks the filename itself (e.g., `the_great_sphinx_of_giza_-_egypt.glb`) in addition to the material name. If either the filename or the material name matches a stone/egyptian asset keyword, it correctly overrides the texture with the common sandstone texture.
